Origin / research question

This opening is a personal starting point, not a finding from the data.

In undergrad, I was on a team that sorted TripAdvisor reviews about visitor harassment by micro-traders — taxi scams, aggressive street vendors — into categories. Later, at Staples, I analyzed exception tickets in Excel to find trends in benefits enrollment. Years later, I started reading Bible stories the same way: as tickets, sorted into categories.

I built those categories from Venn diagrams I drew by hand comparing The Economy of God and The Alchemist. That lexicon became the backbone of this Python analysis.

The Alchemist used to be my bible. It shaped my decisions. Studying it next to Genesis, it started to look like a shadow of Abraham’s story: the same king of Salem, the same tenth, but trust placed in personal dreams rather than in God. Abraham’s kind of faith is the one I want in my own life.

Lead finding: Melchizedek and the tenth

The strongest observation is specific and named in both texts. In Genesis 14:18–20, Melchizedek, king of Salem, blesses Abram, and Abram gives him a tenth of the spoils. In The Alchemist, a king of Salem meets Santiago and charges a tenth of his sheep as a fee. The novel itself compares that fee to Abraham.

just as he had never seen Abraham again after having charged him his one-tenth fee

Paulo Coelho, The Alchemist, translated by Alan R. Clarke (HarperCollins)

That correspondence can support a literary reading: the same recognizable figure stands at a fork between two understandings of a journey. It does not by itself prove that Coelho set out to reverse Genesis.

Five contrasts

Around that figure, five independent contrasts can be read between the Abraham cycle (Genesis 11:26–25:10) and Santiago’s journey. Theological differences that were previously listed separately — calling versus dream, God’s mandate versus omens, trust in God versus desire, and covenant versus Personal Legend — are treated here as one contrast: where trust is placed. Generic travel furniture (tents, stars, the road itself) is omitted. The table is an interpretation, not a claim of design. Alchemist quotations are from Paulo Coelho, The Alchemist, translated by Alan R. Clarke (HarperCollins).

The promise of descendants and land is generational and deferred; Abraham is buried still waiting Genesis 12:2, 12:7, 15:5, 17:8, 25:9–10; Hebrews 11:13 The treasure is personal, and he recovers it in his own lifetime Now he was here not with his flock, but with a shovel. The journeys are organized toward different payoffs: a promise seen from afar, versus a reward claimed by the traveler himself.
Abram is very rich in cattle; the flock is a measure of prosperity Genesis 13:2 The shepherd sells his flock to fund the quest the day he had sold his sheep to pursue his Personal Legend The same pastoral wealth is put to opposite use: kept as a sign of blessing, or liquidated to chase a personal reward.

Abraham-cycle vocabulary

Distinctive-token rate per 10,000 content (non-stopword) tokens. The list is built from Genesis 11:26–25:10, not from Genesis as a whole. These are observations; the controls are not genre-matched (see Limits).

A word counts as distinctive of the Abraham cycle if it occurs at least three times in Genesis 11:26–25:10, its smoothed log-odds versus wordfreq English is at least 4.0 (a heuristic), and it is over-represented against the rest of Genesis (log-odds at least 0.5). Cycle proper names are added, including the hapaxes Melchizedek and Salem; other names must pass the same rest-of-Genesis test. The list has 165 types (78 by log-odds, 87 names-only). Lord, god, and lot are excluded. Egypt, Egyptians, and Pharaoh fall out as more characteristic of later Genesis; Egyptian remains (three tokens in The Alchemist).

On that measure, The Alchemist uses 12 types, 67 tokens, at 40.42 per 10k content tokens, against 3.09 in The Great Gatsby (5 types, 7 tokens) and 11.60 in The Thirty-Nine Steps (10 types, 21 tokens). The novel’s leading items are tent (31), salem (11), camels (9), and melchizedek (4). None of those appear in Gatsby except tent (2). The book also names Abraham once, in connection with Melchizedek’s fee. The gap is real, but Gatsby is a poor control for a desert-quest novel, so the size of the gap should not be read as a measure of biblical influence.

Biblical lexicon

A vocabulary drawn from the King James Bible, with Genesis as the core. The Source column marks terms whose primary biblical citation is Exodus, Leviticus, Job, or 2 Samuel rather than Genesis. Rates are occurrences per 10,000 content tokens.

This table is a frequency inventory, not a proof of symbolism. A word count cannot show whether Coelho uses a term symbolically, typologically, or just as plot furniture. Many high counts simply reflect the story: desert, treasure, crystal, gold, and stone are what the novel is about. The list was also chosen with knowledge of the novel, so there is a selection-bias risk: terms Coelho uses, including gems whose King James source is not Genesis, were included because he uses them. Witness Lee’s definitions, where present, are one typological reading from The Economy of God. They are not the Bible’s own explicit meaning, and a later use of the same word does not inherit that reading automatically.

Limits, method, and reproducibility

Limits

The Great Gatsby and The Thirty-Nine Steps are exploratory controls: public-domain novels of similar era and length, chosen for availability. They do not match The Alchemist’s genre. A comparison set of journey, desert, or spiritual-quest narratives would be the right next step, and without it the frequency gaps should not be treated as surprising evidence of biblical influence. The 4.0 log-odds threshold is a heuristic, not a validated cutoff. Tokenization was spot-checked (noun-only well, the Jordan Baker guard, em-dashes, possessives, and contractions) and is covered by a small test suite.

  1. Isolate the Abraham cycle

    The window is Genesis 11:26–25:10 (377 verses, 9,742 tokens). The rest of Genesis (28,520 tokens) is the contrast set, so Joseph-cycle vocabulary is not treated as Abraham vocabulary. The full King James corpus is 31,102 verses, 790,882 tokens, and 304,848 content tokens.

  2. Score distinctiveness and rate the novels

    Content words need count 3 or more, log-odds versus wordfreq 3.1.1 English of at least 4.0, and log-odds versus the rest of Genesis of at least 0.5. Mid-verse capitalized names are added with a common-name guard; Melchizedek and Salem are force-included. Rates are distinctive tokens per 10,000 content tokens in The Alchemist (39,637 tokens, 16,576 content), The Great Gatsby (49,438; 22,667; Gutenberg #64317), and The Thirty-Nine Steps (41,753; 18,106; Gutenberg #558).

  3. Count a biblical lexicon

    A second list of 72 terms takes Genesis as the core and marks other King James sources per row. Counts are token-sequence matches (regular plurals; Egypt includes Egyptian; gold includes golden; desert and wilderness are one term). Well is counted only in noun-like patterns; Jordan in the novels only in river or plain patterns. Figures are lexical, not sense-tagged.

Reproducibility. Source texts are in the project folder: source/KJV.csv (scrollmapper public-domain King James), data/private/alchemist.txt, data/private/economy.txt, and data/baselines/. Shared tokenization and book cleaners live in textutil.py. From the project folder, run compare.py for Abraham-cycle distinctiveness and book rates, and build_symbolic_genesis.py for the biblical-lexicon counts. The distinctiveness scores need a Python environment with wordfreq 3.1.1. A small test suite checks tokenization, a toy log-odds example, the kjvindex cross-checks, and the Salem and Melchizedek counts. Figures on this page are computed counts from that pipeline, not estimates. The analysis was AI-assisted.
